That cozy candle glow or calming lavender scent can make a room feel cleaner. But is it? Candles, incense, and air fresheners create ambiance and mask odors, yet many of them release hidden pollutants into your indoor air. To see the real impact, we used the [AirBeam Mini](https://www.habitatmap.org/airbeam/buy-it-now/) to measure how these scents change air quality in real time.

The short version: burning candles and incense releases fine particles (PM2.5) into the air you breathe. How much depends on the type of candle, whether it is scented, and how steadily it burns. The good news is that a few simple changes let you enjoy fragrance with far less pollution.

## Are candles bad for your indoor air quality?

Yes, burning candles lowers your indoor air quality, though usually not to dangerous levels in a well-ventilated room. Both paraffin and natural candles release PM2.5 when they burn, and scented candles can also give off volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Enough to move a room from clean into [moderate territory on the air quality scale](https://www.habitatmap.org/blog/what-do-those-colored-circles-mean-understanding-air-quality-on-the-aircasting-map).

Not all candles are equal:

* **Paraffin candles** are petroleum-based and can release VOCs like benzene and toluene as they burn.
* **Natural candles** made from soy, beeswax, or coconut generally burn cleaner, but they still produce some fine particles.
* **Scented candles** add another layer. Their fragrances and dyes can contain synthetic chemicals, allergens, and hormone disruptors like phthalates.

When these compounds burn, they can trigger headaches and irritate the airways. Children, pets, and people with asthma or other respiratory conditions are the most vulnerable.

## Is the air inside your home more polluted than outside?

It can be. The EPA reports that indoor air is often two to five times more polluted than outdoor air.

That is because everyday household items, like gas stoves, candles, fireplaces, furniture, and paints, release gases and fine particles that build up in enclosed spaces. When you picture air pollution, you probably think of traffic or wildfire smoke. But some of the [most harmful air you breathe](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/indoor-pollutants-and-sources) may be inside your own home. Over time, these pollutants can have [lasting effects on your health](https://www.niehs.nih.gov/health/topics/agents/indoor-air).

## Common Sources of Indoor Air Pollution

**1. Gas Stoves**

When gas stoves leak unburned fuel, even with the flame off, that gas can carry toxic chemicals like benzene, toluene, and xylenes. Benzene is a known cancer risk, and the others can affect your nervous system.

Cooking makes it worse. A gas flame releases particulate matter, nitrogen dioxide, carbon monoxide, and formaldehyde. Even short cooking sessions without ventilation can spike indoor pollution and irritate the lungs, especially for children and older adults.

The simplest fix is airflow. Run a range hood or open a window while you cook. A monitor like the AirBeam Mini lets you see these spikes for yourself and check whether your ventilation is actually clearing them.

**2. Candles and Fireplaces**

That cozy glow has a cost. Burning candles and wood releases fine particulate matter (PM2.5), small enough to reach deep into your lungs and bloodstream.

How much depends on how the candle burns. A steady flame emits relatively little. A flickering, sooty, or smoldering flame emits far more, which is why drafty rooms and unstable flames are the worst case.

In our own testing, lighting candles and running a fireplace produced clear, sudden spikes in PM2.5.

**3. Furniture, Paints, and Fabrics**

Many household materials release volatile organic compounds (VOCs), invisible gases that can off-gas for months or even years. Common examples are formaldehyde and benzene, which can cause headaches, allergic reactions, and long-term respiratory irritation.

Not every pollutant is visible, but both gases and particles affect your health. Over time, exposure can contribute to allergies, respiratory issues, and chronic inflammation.

## What happens when you actually measure it

Real-time measurement makes the invisible visible. Published research shows how much everyday scents add to indoor air. A controlled [2025 study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-95010-0) found that a single scented candle raised nearby PM2.5 to roughly 26 to 31 µg/m³ within half an hour. Incense tends to be worse, and studies consistently rank it among the strongest indoor sources of fine particles.

In our own testing with the AirBeam Mini, we saw the same pattern. A single paraffin candle in a small living room sent particle levels up quickly. Incense pushed them higher still. Even natural soy candles, which burn cleaner, produced detectable spikes.

The lesson is not that you must give up scents. It is that burning things indoors sends up sudden bursts of tiny particles that linger in the air, and over time they can make allergies and breathing problems worse.

## How to enjoy scents safely

The goal is not to fear every candle. It is to enjoy fragrance with less pollution. A few simple habits help:

* Choose natural candles like beeswax or soy over paraffin.
* Keep the flame steady and trim the wick to reduce soot.
* Try no-burn options like dried lavender, essential oils, or simmer pots.
* Open a window or run a fan to keep air moving.
* Monitor your indoor air so you know what is actually working.

Even small changes make a real difference.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are candles bad for you?** Burning candles releases fine particles (PM2.5) that can irritate the lungs, though in a well-ventilated room the levels are usually moderate rather than dangerous.

**Do soy candles pollute less than paraffin?** Soy and beeswax candles generally burn cleaner than paraffin, but they still produce some fine particulate matter.

**Is burning incense worse than candles?** Research consistently finds incense to be a stronger source of PM2.5 than most candles, so it tends to raise indoor pollution more.

**How can I make my home smell nice without polluting the air?** No-burn options like essential oils, dried flowers, and simmer pots add fragrance without combustion, and keeping windows open helps clear any particles.
